Abstract 
This thesis documents how I co-founded and scaled Glam Up, an AI beauty app, to over 
one million users (and $150K MRR) between May and November 2024 — without paid ads, 
funding, or a pre-existing audience. Built on live experimentation and behavioral psychology, 
this paper breaks down how my co-founder and I engineered virality through required referrals, 
TikTok-optimized content loops, and emotionally timed monetization. Drawing on user data 
from App Store Developer Console, PostHog and Superwall, I analyze what drove conversions, 
what failed, and how content became our distribution engine. I also reflect on the mindset behind 
building fast-moving consumer apps — staying scrappy, being aware, and knowing when it’s 
time to walk away. This isn’t a universal playbook, but a tactical case study of what actually 
worked, what didn’t, and how delusional faith, smart systems, and emotional insight helped scale 
one of the fastest-growing Gen Z apps of 2024. If you're building in the AI consumer space, I 
hope this gives you something useful to learn from.

1. Introduction 
Glam Up is an AI beauty consultant app that helps users discover their color analysis, 
ideal makeup products, facial features, and personalized beauty routines through facial scanning. 
It launched in May 2024 and reached over one million users within six months. 
As the co-founder of Glam Up, I was intimately involved in everything it took to grow 
the app from a scrappy idea to one of the fastest-growing AI beauty apps of the year. I wasn’t a 
seasoned entrepreneur with years of experience in venture-backed startups, nor did I have a 
massive marketing budget to throw at paid ads. What I did have, however, was spontaneity, 
delusional faith, and the grit to figure things out as I went. 
This thesis analyzes the strategies we used to scale Glam Up — referral loops, TikTok 
virality, paywall psychology, and creator content — and how each impacted user growth and 
monetization. While B2C consumer apps are often seen as riskier and harder to monetize, Glam 
Up challenged that assumption. B2B startups consistently receive a larger share of VC funding, 
with reports showing that over 70% of early-stage capital in recent years has flowed toward

3. Literature Review
The virality and monetization of Glam Up sit at the intersection of behavioral 
psychology, marketing strategy, and user-centered product design. While traditional tech 
literature often overlooks consumer AI apps, newer studies help explain how and why apps like 
Glam Up succeed. This section draws from research in four key areas: unconventional startup 
pathways, influencer marketing effectiveness, pricing in freemium apps, and intellectual property 
as a growth and defense tool. 
Lean startup methodology has long dominated startup education (Ries, 2011). The core 
idea is simple: build fast, test constantly, and iterate based on real-world feedback. Glam Up 
followed this model almost by accident. We didn’t start with a polished vision or a five-year 
roadmap — we started with a 10-minute phone call and a rough idea. From there, everything was 
tested live: onboarding flows, pricing, referral systems, and TikTok content. Bingham and 
Eisenhardt (2011) find that in high-velocity environments, flexible experimentation outperforms 


--- PAGE 10 ---
9 
structured planning. Their work supports the idea that spontaneous, iterative growth can 
outperform well-funded but rigid startups. Moreover, Eisenmann et al. (2012) argue that 
early-stage consumer startups are better served by hypotheses and rapid testing than by 
long-term financial projections, especially in Gen Z markets where tastes shift quickly. The 
spontaneous build-then-iterate model isn’t a liability; it’s an advantage when targeting cultural 
trends and platforms like TikTok. Glam Up’s ability to move fast, experiment often, and double 
down on what worked wasn’t just a strategy – it was a necessity. In the fast-moving world of 
consumer AI and TikTok-driven trends, that flexibility became our biggest advantage. 
While Glam Up was built through quick testing and gut instinct, most consumer apps 
grow by leaning on social trust, especially social media marketing. Influencer marketing 
effectiveness has been studied extensively in the context of consumer behavior. Lou and Yuan 
(2019) demonstrate that influencer trustworthiness and similarity to the viewer increase brand 
credibility and purchase intent. While influencer marketing didn’t work directly for Glam Up, 
our shift from macro-influencers to relatable UGC creators and faceless beauty accounts was 
successful (discussed in Section 7). De Veirman et al. (2017) found that influencer follower 
count is inversely related to trust when controlling for engagement. In other words, 
micro-influencers, especially those who appear non-commercial, can drive higher conversion 
because users perceive their recommendations as genuine. This explains why Glam Up’s 
aesthetic, faceless content generated more shares and conversions than traditional influencer 
campaigns. Further, Labrecque (2014) highlights the role of parasocial interaction — the 
one-sided psychological relationships people form with media figures. These interactions 
increase emotional attachment and explain why emotionally framed TikToks convert better than 
generic explainer content. 


--- PAGE 11 ---
10 
Freemium models have been dissected in dozens of pricing studies, especially in mobile 
gaming and wellness apps. Yang et al. (2020) provide empirical support that conversion rates 
increase when apps delay monetization until users experience functional value. This supports 
Glam Up’s decision to delay the paywall until after the facial scanning animation, leveraging 
anticipation and commitment bias to increase conversions (discussed in Section 6). Tversky and 
Kahneman (1991) developed the loss aversion principle: people are more motivated to avoid 
losing something than to gain something of equal value. Glam Up applied this through its 
post-paywall discount tactic—users were first offered full price, rejected it, and then faced a 50% 
discount (a framed “loss” if ignored). Ariely (2008) adds that price anchoring—seeing a high 
price first—makes subsequent prices feel more reasonable. Seufert (2022) also emphasizes the 
importance of multi-step monetization flows in consumer apps, where users are primed over 
several interactions instead of being asked to pay upfront. This strategy is known to improve 
both revenue and perceived value. Combining behavioral economics with timing and emotional 
buildup allowed Glam Up to maintain viral growth without sacrificing monetization—a rare 
balance in freemium models. 
In the consumer space, IP isn’t just about legal protection—it’s about building defensible 
visibility. Gans and Stern (2019) argue that for digital startups, the strongest protection comes 
not from patents but from speed, branding, and network effects. This aligns with Glam Up’s 
strategy of dominating TikTok and Instagram feeds with visually recognizable, emotionally 
resonant scan results. While copyright and trademarks offer some protection (WIPO, 2021), the 
literature shows that perceived ownership of a user experience is often more powerful. Varian 
(1998) explains that in the digital age, information goods like apps derive their value from 
attention and usage, not scarcity. This means the most viral apps win not by locking others out, 


--- PAGE 12 ---
11 
but by staying top-of-mind and culturally embedded. Apps like Poparazzi, Clubhouse, and 
BeReal all saw copycats—yet the original brands maintained dominance due to faster iteration 
and stronger community identification. Glam Up followed the same trajectory, differentiating 
itself with a content engine and aesthetic that were difficult to replicate at scale. We didn’t rely 
on patents or trademarks to protect Glam Up. Instead, we were driven by the unique visual 
language we created, from scan animations to Figma-branded results. That content signature 
made Glam Up recognizable and hard to replicate, even as competitors mimicked our 
functionality.

5. Incentivizing Referrals 
In AI consumer apps, the default growth playbook is often built around paid ads and 
influencer deals. Brands pour money into Instagram shoutouts, TikTok collabs, and app install 
campaigns to get initial traction — but it rarely lasts. According to a 2023 report by Business of 
Apps, the average cost per install in the beauty app category exceeds $3, and customer 
acquisition costs (CAC) tend to climb as competition intensifies. For a bootstrapped startup like 
ours, that kind of spend wasn’t just risky—it was impossible. 
So we asked ourselves: what if virality wasn’t a bonus, but a requirement? 
Our core hypothesis was simple: if users were encouraged to share the app in order to 
unlock features—or offered a clear tradeoff between paying or referring—we could transform 
user necessity into a viral loop. Instead of relying on passive word-of-mouth, we built a system 
where every new user could unlock their first scan by either subscribing or referring three 
friends. In other words, growth wouldn’t be a bonus, but a part of the product’s functionality. 
At the time, we didn’t know how powerful TikTok would become in amplifying this loop. 
It started as a secondary channel — we weren’t banking on virality there, but we knew from my 
background in content and trends that it was worth experimenting with. What followed was a 


--- PAGE 14 ---
13 
series of unexpected but measurable boosts from TikTok that supercharged the referral system 
far beyond what we initially modeled. 
This section explores the design of that referral-based growth system — what we 
hypothesized, how we tested it, and the unexpected role TikTok played in accelerating user 
acquisition, paywall conversions, and overall viral momentum. 
5.1 How We Engineered Virality: Referral Loops + TikTok 
When we first launched Glam Up, we knew we couldn’t rely on ads. We didn’t have the 
budget, and even if we did, the economics wouldn’t have worked. So instead of spending money 
to acquire users, we made growth part of the product experience itself. Users had two options: 
either pay for a weekly subscription or refer three friends to unlock their first scan. Unlike most 
apps that treat referrals as optional or hidden behind settings menus, we made them central to the 
product. You couldn’t proceed without choosing — either you pay, or you help us grow. 
Let’s look at what actually happened: 
● In October 2024 alone, Glam Up gained 290,518 new users, defined here as users who 
opened the app and began engaging with its onboarding experience. 
● 90% of those users reached the paywall, meaning they selected a scan, uploaded a photo, 
and stayed through the lead-up sequence. That’s 261,672 users hitting the paywall. 
● Out of those, 15,150 converted into paying subscribers, giving us a conversion rate of 
5.8%. 
This 5.8% conversion rate reflects only the users who reached the paywall — not total 
app downloads — and it's actually a strong result by consumer app standards. Most freemium 
mobile apps convert just 1–4% of users into subscribers (Clevertap, 2022), while top-performing 
apps in the beauty and scanning category — like Lensa AI, Facetune, and Remini — typically 


--- PAGE 15 ---
14 
see 4–7% conversion rates during periods of peak performance. Compared to those benchmarks, 
Glam Up’s October performance was well within a healthy range. 
Despite relying purely on organic growth, Glam Up generated $77,955 in new revenue 
that month alone. This wasn’t driven by ad spend or influencer hype — it was driven by a deeply 
optimized funnel and emotionally-timed conversion moments. The volume of users reaching the 
paywall, combined with strong intent and minimal friction, allowed us to monetize at a level 
most early-stage consumer apps struggle to reach.

Over the past decade, consumer apps have generally followed one of two viral growth 
paths: (1) social-driven growth, typically powered by platforms like TikTok or Instagram, or (2) 
invite-based loops that rely on exclusivity and referrals. Few apps have successfully combined 
both in a way that compounds. 
Apps like BeReal and Poparazzi scaled rapidly by leaning into TikTok trends and cultural 
relevance, but they never required referrals or sharing to unlock value (Shepard, 2022). On the 
other hand, Clubhouse and Robinhood grew by using invite-only models to build hype and social 
proof, especially in their early stages (Molla, 2021). However, they didn’t capitalize on 
algorithmic amplification. 
Glam Up approached growth differently: we treated TikTok and referrals as a unified 
system. TikTok was the awareness engine, and referrals converted that attention into user 
acquisition. When TikTok content went viral, it didn’t just drive installs—it directly accelerated 
referrals, creating a flywheel of visibility and action. 
This connection became especially clear between October 19–23, when our trained UGC 
creators posted a wave of optimized videos showcasing scan features. These videos triggered a 
sharp rise in referral-driven signups, from 38% to 52%, in just four days. Unlike the July spike,

TikTok’s algorithm further amplified this effect. The For You Page prioritizes content

which revealed that TikTok ranks videos based heavily on user interactions such as likes, 
comments, and watch time (Zhong & Frenkel, 2021). Since many users commented their referral

Ultimately, TikTok was not just a traffic source—it was the fuel that powered our referral 
engine. Without it, the loop wouldn’t have scaled. But without the loop, the awareness wouldn’t 
have converted. By tightly integrating top-of-funnel content with built-in virality mechanics, 
Glam Up turned organic social media into a self-sustaining growth system. 
5.3 What This Taught Us About Viral Growth 
Looking back, what made Glam Up grow wasn’t one tactic. It was how everything 
clicked together. The referral system gave people a reason to share. The scan experience gave 
them something to talk about. And TikTok gave it all a stage. 
What surprised us most was how little of it felt like traditional growth marketing. There 
were no ad budgets. No massive influencer campaigns. Just a feedback loop that turned users 
into promoters and curiosity into motion. We didn’t force virality. We just structured the product 
so that users had something they wanted to spread, and a reason to do it. 
We also realized that virality doesn’t have to come from pure novelty. It can come from 
necessity. People didn’t share Glam Up just because it was cool. They shared it because they had

20 
to — to unlock the scan, to see their results, to satisfy their curiosity. It wasn’t “growth hacking.” 
It was a growth design. 
That insight shaped everything that came next. It made us rethink how to monetize 
(Section 2), how to build product features, and even how to work with creators. We didn’t just 
want attention. We wanted momentum. And that meant designing every part of the user journey 
— from first download to first referral — with psychology, timing, and social behavior in mind. 
 
6. Monetization 
When we thought about how to monetize Glam Up, we weren’t trying to build a perfect 
pricing model from day one. We just wanted something simple that fit the way users actually 
behaved. A lot of AI consumer apps either monetize too early or push expensive plans without 
emotional timing. For Glam Up, we chose to design monetization around curiosity, impulse, and 
lightweight choices. This section breaks down what that looked like: our pricing decisions, the 
psychology behind the paywall, the surprise impact of raising prices, and what actually drove 
revenue at scale. 
6.1 User Flow and Paywall Design

2. Welcome screens 
3. Onboarding questions 
4. “Leave a rating” page 
5. “Analyzing your results” loading page 
6. Home screen: select a scan

7. Upload or take a selfie

Step 1: Open the app 
Glam Up launches immediately with no login screen, no “sign in with Google,” and no 
request for email or phone number. This was a strategic decision to eliminate all friction between 
the user and the core product. By removing account creation, we ensured that the highest number 
of users possible made it through to the paywall. The idea was simple: if someone’s curious 
enough to download the app, they should be able to get started without second-guessing. 
Momentum bias, the tendency for people to continue something once they’ve started, works in 
our favor here. Literature on freemium mobile apps (Yang et al., 2020; Seufert, 2022) supports 
this approach, showing that early friction like login screens or data capture significantly reduces 
conversion in products where emotional payoff happens later in the funnel. We didn’t want 
emails. We wanted users to get hooked. 
Step 2: Welcome screens 
After opening, users are greeted with three aesthetic welcome pages: “Glow up now,” 
“Discover your features,” and “Built by experts.” Each page features soft pink animated icons 
with short, encouraging captions. The visuals were intentionally designed to feel like a modern 
beauty brand — clean, minimal, and aspirational. This was our first chance to shape perception. 
We didn’t throw features at the user; we created a vibe. The goal here wasn’t education — it was 


--- PAGE 23 ---
22 
alignment. We wanted users to feel like this app got them, that it came from the same world as 
the beauty content they already trust. 
Step 3: Onboarding questions 
The onboarding flow follows immediately after the welcome screens. Users answer a 
short series of questions about their age, gender, beauty goals, skin concerns, and product 
preferences. Although none of these answers actually affect the scan results, they serve an 
important psychological purpose. By asking users to reflect on what they care about (whether 
they want to glow up, what types of products they use, or what concerns they have), we’re 
priming them to become emotionally invested in whatever results we show next. This also subtly 
introduces the idea that Glam Up understands their individual needs, even though the backend 
doesn’t yet tailor anything. It’s all about perceived personalization — the feeling that this app 
isn’t just for “beauty,” but for you. 
Step 4: “Leave a rating” page 
Before users can proceed to the core product, they’re shown a screen that reads: “Leave a 
rating! We’re a small team, so a rating goes a really long way!” The only available button is 
“Leave a rating,” which triggers the App Store review prompt. Once that closes, regardless of 
whether the user actually submitted a review, a second button appears: “I rated.” This is the only 
way to continue. This design introduces a subtle psychological tension: users who didn’t leave a 
rating must still click “I rated” to continue, effectively nudging them into either completing the 
review or momentarily “lying” to move forward. That moment of hesitation is intentional. It 
plays on consistency bias – the tendency for people to align their actions with prior choices in 
order to appear internally consistent (Cialdini, 2009) – and on moral friction – the discomfort 
people feel when their behavior doesn’t align with their self-image as moral (Mazar et al., 2008). 


--- PAGE 24 ---
23 
Users, when faced with this choice, may go back and rate the app simply to feel honest. The 
request is low-effort, the guilt is minimal, and the placement (before any true product value is 
revealed) ensures that users are emotionally neutral and unlikely to push back. Framing it as a 
favor to a “small team” also taps into reciprocity bias, a well-documented social norm where 
people feel obligated to return a favor (Cialdini, 2009). Whether or not they actually submit a 
rating, the user walks away from the interaction feeling helpful and slightly more invested in 
what comes next. 
Step 5: “Analyzing your results” loading page 
After the rating screen, users arrive at a fake loading page that reads “Analyzing your 
results.” This screen appears for 5 seconds before revealing results, which are actually 
pre-determined based on onboarding answers. The real goal isn’t analysis. It’s suspense. This 
moment primes users to believe the app is doing something technically complex and valuable. 
Simulated computation like this is common in consumer apps, especially those rooted in AI or 
personalization. Research in human-computer interaction shows that when users experience a 
delay, even artificial, they perceive the result as more accurate and meaningful (Gray & Wegner, 
2013; Langer et al., 1978). The act of waiting creates the illusion of depth, triggering the effort 
justification effect, the tendency to assign greater value to outcomes that required effort or delay 
(Aronson & Mills, 1959). It turns curiosity into anticipation and boosts perceived legitimacy. 
Step 6: Home screen – select a scan 
Once the fake analysis screen completes, users arrive at the home screen and are 
prompted to select one of three scans: Color Analysis, Glow Up Guide, or Facial Analysis. Each 
scan option includes a visual icon to help the user decide. This moment gives users a feeling of 
control, like they’re choosing their own path. This perceived autonomy has been shown to 


--- PAGE 25 ---
24 
increase emotional investment and satisfaction, even when the choices are superficial or lead to 
the same outcome (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000). Users also may feel like they’re customizing their 
experience, even though every flow ultimately leads to the same core conversion point. 
Step 7: Upload or take a selfie 
After choosing a scan, users are asked to either take a photo or upload a selfie from their 
gallery. This is a powerful psychological moment: when a user uploads their own face, they cross 
a line between casual interest and personal involvement. There’s now something at stake. 
They’ve made the experience about themselves. By placing this moment before the paywall, not 
after, we ensured that the user had already emotionally committed to the process. It was no 
longer a generic app; it was a mirror. 
Step 8: Fake scan animation plays 
Once the selfie is submitted, a scanning animation begins. But nothing is actually being 
processed yet. This scan is 100% fake, but the user doesn’t know that. The animation wasn’t 
added just for polish, but to make the moment feel real. We wanted the user to believe that 
something technical and valuable had just happened.  
Step 9: Blurred result pages are shown 
After the animation, the user is shown a multi-part results screen, but everything is 
blurred. The “Changes Needed” page is blurred. The personalized product list is blurred. Even 
the user’s selfie is blurred. This is the emotional cliffhanger. The user can see that something’s 
there, but they can’t access it. And because they’ve already spent a few minutes answering 
questions, taking a photo, and watching a scan, they feel like they’ve earned the results. The 
blurring creates tension. The ambiguity keeps them moving. We designed the page to feel 
complete, but just out of reach.  


--- PAGE 26 ---
25 
Step 10: Paywall/Referral choice is presented 
At the bottom of the blurred screen, two buttons appear: “See Your Results” or “Invite 3 
Friends.” This is the moment of conversion, but we didn’t frame it as a subscription offer. We 
framed it as a natural next step in the journey the user already started. Either you pay to see your 
results now, or you refer friends and unlock it for free. Both paths let the user feel like they’re 
continuing something they already began instead of starting something new. That distinction 
matters. 
Step 11: Swiping paywall videos appear 
If the user taps “See Your Results,” they’re shown a three-part swiping video carousel, 
each designed to build emotional momentum, trust, and urgency. The first video is tailored 
specifically to the scan they chose. For example, if the user selected the Color Analysis Scan, 
they would see an animation video that mimics the actual scanning process, showing 
personalized color season & palette, colors to avoid, etc. This scan-specific video is there to 
re-trigger their curiosity and subtly reaffirm that they will get their results. 
The second video is pure social proof: a fast-cut montage of creators showing the app, 
reacting to their results, and showing Glam Up like “I use this app a lot” or “wait this is insane”. 
We pulled these from our past influencer collaborations but stripped away their branding so they 
felt more like random people posting on TikTok. Overlaid with the caption “Viral on TikTok,” 
this video is designed to say: A LOT of people just like you are using this app and loving it.  
The third video is a vertical scroll-through of dozens of 5-star app reviews that we 
designed directly in Figma. These reviews were crafted to reflect the most common emotional 
pain points: confusion at Sephora, spending so much money on random products, feeling like 
makeup never looks right, etc. The tone was distinctly Gen Z, full of slang, emojis, and broken 


--- PAGE 27 ---
26 
punctuation to feel like something you'd read on TikTok. The design was to show 1) high ratings 
and 2) Glam Up users feel like their own struggles were being understood and solved by Glam 
Up. 
Each of these three videos serves a specific purpose: the first re-activates interest and 
builds perceived product legitimacy, the second creates social validation and urgency through 
TikTok virality cues, and the third hits emotional relatability and trust. Together, they form a 
layered persuasion sequence that doesn’t feel like a sales pitch. 
Step 12: User decision point 
Finally, the user decides: pay, refer, or leave. But by this point, nearly every 
psychological lever has already been pulled. They’ve emotionally committed, invested effort, 
seen social validation, and built up anticipation for a result that feels uniquely theirs. We weren’t 
selling a scan. We were offering closure. And for many users, that’s what made the price feel 
worth it. 
6.2 Pricing 
When we first started thinking about how to monetize Glam Up, we didn’t 
overcomplicate it. We had seen a few viral consumer apps start using weekly subscriptions 
instead of the more traditional monthly or annual plans. This trend is reflected in recent pricing 
reports that show increased adoption of short-cycle plans in mobile AI and wellness apps 
(Clevertap, 2022). It wasn’t common yet, but it stood out. The format felt fast, emotional, and 
lightweight, which aligned with how our users made decisions — it feels more like buying a 
coffee or a lip gloss than committing to a subscription. That small psychological distinction 
mattered. We weren’t trying to sell users a long-term membership. We were trying to convert a 


--- PAGE 28 ---
27 
moment of curiosity into a one-time “why not?” purchase. Weekly pricing gave us a format that 
matched the impulsive, aesthetic, and emotionally driven mindset we were building for. 
Once we locked in the weekly model, we had to decide how much to charge — and 
again, we looked around. At the time, other scan-based or AI-driven apps were pricing weekly 
subscriptions anywhere between $1.99 and $6.99. We decided to start on the lower end: 
$2.99/week for one scan, $6.99/week for two scans, and $8.99/week for four. Our thinking was 
pretty straightforward — we were a new app, we didn’t have brand trust yet, and we wanted the 
price to feel accessible. If someone was on the fence, we didn’t want cost to be the reason they 
bounced. Especially since the entire flow leading up to the paywall was designed to build 
emotional momentum, it felt important that the price didn’t interrupt that rhythm. We didn’t want 
people thinking, “Wait, is this worth it?” We wanted them to think, “It’s only a few bucks—I 
might as well try it.” 
6.3 Emotional Timing, Price Increases, and Scan Upgrades 
One of the things we added early on (and honestly it was kind of sneaky lol) was the 
second paywall. After users went through the scan and saw the full-price screen, if they clicked 
away, we immediately hit them with a 50% discounted version of the same offer. From the user’s 
perspective, it felt like they unlocked something by saying no. And that was exactly the point. 
They’d just seen $6.99. Now it was $3.49. That contrast alone created this instinctive “Oh 
wait that’s way cheaper” reaction. It reframed the value of the app without us having to explain 
anything. Psychologically, it hit all the right levers: anchoring (the higher price made the 
discount feel better), loss aversion (they were about to walk away from something they were 
already invested in), and impulse timing (we caught them right as curiosity peaked). 


--- PAGE 29 ---
28 
At the time, we didn’t fully realize how powerful that moment would be. We just thought, 
“Let’s show a cheaper price and see if it helps.” But it ended up becoming one of our strongest 
conversion tactics, especially because users were already a few minutes deep by that point. 
They’d taken a selfie, answered onboarding questions, and watched the scan animation. They 
were bought in emotionally. And when you’re in that state, the sudden appearance of a better 
deal feels like a small win. 
In July, we decided to raise prices. Honestly we didn’t have massive data insight or a 
proper reason to do it. It was one of those "fuck it” moments. We raised the weekly prices to 
$8.99, $10.99, and $12.99 for one, two, and four scans, while still keeping the intro offers in 
place for the first week. At the time, we were fully expecting conversion rates to drop. That’s the 
usual logic—raise prices, see fewer signups, and hope the revenue balances out. We were 
preparing ourselves for that trade-off. But then… it didn’t happen. In fact, conversions started 
going up. Not dramatically at first, but consistently. And by the time we hit November, we were 
sitting at a 5.5% average conversion rate—higher than before the price increase.

the app: casually, playfully, and often after seeing a TikTok that inspired them to try something

What I’ve learned from all of this is that monetization isn’t just about pricing models or 
where you place the paywall. It’s about timing, framing, and how people feel in the moment 
they’re deciding to pay. For Glam Up, weekly pricing likely worked not because it was the most 
strategic plan on paper, but because it matched the emotional rhythm of our users. It felt casual.

7.3 Things that didn’t work 
Now that we’ve covered what worked, I think it’s even more important to talk about what 
didn’t. For every viral video and growth loop that scaled, there were many things that flopped, 
underperformed, or wasted time and money. Here’s the stuff we tested—and won’t be doing 
again anytime soon. 
1. Influencer Marketing: A Whole Lot of Effort for Nothing 
We started with the obvious: influencer marketing. I spent weeks building out email 
campaigns and outreach databases, trying every tool — Modash, Upfluence, Collabstr, and 
scraping TikTok bios manually. 
Our hit rate was already low, but even when we booked influencers, the results were 
underwhelming. One creator with 150K followers charged up to $10,000 for a single video. It 
would get 100K views and maybe 200 users. Others may just completely ghost after signing an 
agreement.  
We also discovered the platform tooling problem. Tools like Modash are dominated by 
agency-affiliated influencers — people who charge 5–10x more because they have 
representation. We ended up manually filtering creators by email domain just to find non-agency 
ones (look for @gmail or @outlook = usually safe). But even that didn’t guarantee results. 
Many influencers also didn’t know how to talk about apps. They knew how to talk about 
beauty products, Amazon finds, or daily routines, but converting a swipe into a download and 
referral was a different game. The call to action was unclear, and many didn’t show the scan or 
results at all. The content looked like an ad, and it didn’t spark curiosity or emotion. 
However, I want to mention that influencer marketing can work, especially for brands 
and lifestyle products. It has worked well for apps like Finch or BeReal, which relied on

2. Faceless Accounts: Cheap Reach Isn’t Enough 
Our first real moment of traction came from a faceless TikTok post. A creator we paid 
just $10 posted a simple tutorial about Glam Up, and hit over 1 million views. That single post 
kicked off a viral loop that brought in thousands of new users, and for a brief moment, it made 
faceless beauty accounts look like the perfect growth strategy. 
But as we quickly learned, that post was an outlier. 
Encouraged by the results, we tried to scale the approach across both Instagram and 
TikTok. On Instagram, we partnered with over 50 faceless beauty pages — accounts that post 
aesthetic content with no personal brand. We paid $5–$15 per post and were able to quickly build 
a network of accounts pushing Glam Up content. These pages got decent engagement; some 
posts reached 500K+ views. But conversions were low. Particularly on Instagram, most of their

Instagram’s faceless account space was cheap and easy to access, but the quality of the 
traffic didn’t align with our monetization goals. Most of the beauty accounts on Instagram are 
actually run by Indian men, resourceful hustlers who saw Instagram as a side hustle. They didn’t

engagement. The algorithm rewards momentum, but these accounts were too volatile to build

By late 2024, saturation made things worse. Dozens of other apps started flooding the 
same faceless accounts. AI makeup apps, color analysis app, motivational apps were all flodding 
into the Instagram faceless account space and driving the market rate up. The audience also got 
overwhelmed. Engagement dropped. And what once looked like organic beauty content started 
to feel like back-to-back sponsored spam. 
In the end, faceless accounts — both on TikTok and Instagram — played an important 
role in Glam Up’s early days. They helped us test formats, seed content, and hit our first viral 
spike. But they weren’t scalable, repeatable, or reliable. They offered reach, but not revenue. 
Visibility, but not trust. 
Real growth didn’t happen when we rented distribution from creators we didn’t know. It 
happened when we built our own engine — one we could control, optimize, and scale. 
3. Celebrity Glow Up Content 
We ran several TikTok slideshows and video posts featuring celebrity transformations, 
like “What’s Zendaya’s color palette?” or “Taylor Swift before vs. after having her beauty 
consultants.” These videos looked great and often pulled in decent reach — anywhere from 10K 
to 50K views. But they rarely converted. People watched, maybe even saved or liked the post, 
but didn’t follow through to download the app. These posts felt more like entertainment than 
activation. They didn’t spark that key emotional reaction of “this could be me.” There was no 
personal relevance or urgency, just curiosity at best. That said, celebrity-based content still has a 
place. It can help establish brand credibility and aesthetics. Some of our best top-of-funnel 
videos included celebrity references and performed well from a visibility standpoint. It just 
didn’t translate to conversions for us.  


--- PAGE 47 ---
46 
Apps like Lensa AI succeeded with celebrity-style content because they didn’t stop at 
showing famous faces—they gave users the tools to see themselves in that same glow-up 
narrative. Their success wasn’t just about aesthetics; it was about personalization and 
participation. By combining high-impact visual effects with shareable outputs and viral 
mechanics, like posting your own “Magic Avatar” or AI-enhanced version. They made the 
experience feel aspirational and actionable. People weren’t just admiring someone else’s 
transformation; they were actively engaging in their own. 
That’s where our celebrity content fell short. Without that same interactive hook or 
identity-based outcome, our slideshows became just another pretty video. They sparked passive 
curiosity, but not emotional urgency. There was no “I need to try this now” moment. And on 
TikTok, where content moves fast and emotional triggers matter, that’s the difference between a 
scroll-past and a download. 
For celebrity content to truly convert, it has to go beyond recognition. It has to invite 
users to imagine themselves in that transformation. Without that personal payoff, it doesn’t 
matter how beautiful or well-produced the content is. It won’t drive meaningful growth. 
7.4 The Value of What Didn’t Work 
Looking back, none of these failures were wasted. Each misstep taught us something 
foundational. These experiments revealed what didn’t work for us, at our stage, with our users. 
And that clarity is priceless. 
We didn’t learn by sitting in a strategy room guessing what might work. We learned by 
trying, failing, and adjusting quickly. That $10 TikTok post didn’t happen because we were 
smart—it happened because we were willing to test everything until something clicked. And 


--- PAGE 48 ---
47 
once it clicked, we didn’t romanticize the discovery. We broke it down, rebuilt it into a system, 
and scaled it deliberately. 
In consumer apps, especially ones built around identity and aesthetics like Glam Up, 
growth doesn't come from copying trends or hiring expensive creators. It comes from deep 
alignment between product, psychology, and content format. None of the flops detailed here 
were bad ideas in general—they just weren’t the right fit for this app, at this time, with this 
audience. 
 
8. What Next? 
After Glam Up’s viral moment, after the 150K MRR and 40~50 million views in 
November 2024, we didn’t try to push it further. Not really.  
After Glam Up’s initial virality, we maintained ~$100K MRR for about two months 
before revenue began to taper. By March 2025, our MRR stabilized at around $40K, largely 
driven by continued views from past viral content. Interestingly, many of our most successful 
TikToks didn’t die out after a few weeks. They’ve continued to accumulate views over time, 
keeping our acquisition pipeline passively alive even without new experiments. We’ve 
maintained a consistent posting schedule with creators, but haven’t launched major new 
campaigns since November 2024. 
During this time, over 10 similar beauty AI apps launched, many of them clearly inspired 
by Glam Up. But none, aside from the brief confusion-driven virality of Glam AI, have achieved 
meaningful traction or cultural relevance. The space feels saturated now, and while the concept 
still draws curiosity, the novelty that once drove explosive growth is fading.

There were obvious next steps. We could’ve scaled the content engine, built a 100-person 
UGC army, and leaned into every growth lever available to consumer apps in this space. We 
talked about launching an affiliate program. We had early conversations with major brands in 
beauty and skincare who were excited to collaborate. I even reached out to Charli D’Amelio’s 
talent agency – multiple managers replied and were genuinely down to explore celebrity-backed 
content partnerships. 
We also explored the monetization path through product recommendations. Since Glam 
Up was already generating personalized beauty routines and AI-powered product lists, it made 
sense to integrate with retailers or brands directly. A few big names were open to the idea. If we 
had moved faster, we probably could’ve built an affiliate-based DTC channel or partnered with 
legacy beauty brands looking for a Gen Z edge. 
But... we didn’t. 
We didn’t double down. We didn’t raise funding. We didn’t hire a growth team or chase 
partnerships. And the truth is: I think both me and my co-founder were just tired. Not burnt out 
exactly, but we missed the 0 to 1 phase. The part where you’re figuring something out for the 
first time, not trying to optimize what’s already working. The messy, uncertain, late-night energy 
of making something out of nothing. 
Once the formula was working, it became… less fun. Less creative. More like running a 
playbook than building something new. 
And honestly, part of it was the business model itself. Glam Up wasn’t built to retain — it 
was built to explode. Users paid per scan, which meant revenue was front-loaded. But that also 
meant they had little reason to keep coming back after a few tries. To become a truly sticky, 
long-term app, we would’ve needed to invest way more into engineering and product: better

community features. At some point, we started seeing Glam Up more like a

attention and cash, not long-term habit. Turning it into a sustainable project was doable. But to 
us, it wasn’t really worth building — especially if we could already use Glam Up as a cash cow 
to fund the next project.  
And personally, I didn’t want to wake up and spend my day negotiating brand deals, 
managing creators, or navigating celebrity managers' calendars. I didn’t want to turn Glam Up 
into a CPG tool or a content house. Even though it could’ve been a solid business. Even though 
we had traction most people would kill for. 
So… We stopped scaling before the sprint turned into a marathon we didn’t want to run. 
There’s something addictive about early-stage building. When every day feels like it 
could be the day something takes off. When you’re testing 10 ideas a week, pulling late nights 
because you're curious, not obligated. That’s what I loved. Not maintaining a system, but making 
sense of chaos. 
Glam Up gave me that in spades. It taught me how consumer apps grow, what virality 
feels like, how users behave, and how fast everything can change when you get the right 
moment. But it also taught me to pay attention to my own energy. What kind of builder I want to 
be. What kind of problems I want to solve. 
After stepping back from Glam Up, we built again. But this time, my co-founder and I 
didn’t want to just run one app. We wanted to build a system that could launch many. A 
repeatable infrastructure for going from idea → prototype → traction — fast. An app studio! One

That’s how Prep AI started. 
Prep AI is "Tinder” for jobs – swipe on jobs and we automatically apply for you on the

just hit our first viral loop. We’re sitting at ~$10K MRR and climbing fast. 
A lot of what’s working now are strategies I’ve already shared in this thesis: emotional 
hooks, thoughtful onboarding sequence, performance-based UGCs, and viral mechanics that feel 
native to the platform. It’s surreal watching the same playbook come to life in a totally different 
vertical. 
Different product, same energy: start fast, test hard, stay close to the user, and let the 
internet surprise you! 
 
9. Playbook and Mindset 
Most people overcomplicate virality. They think it’s about luck, timing, or having the 
perfect product. It’s not. Going viral is a system, and staying viral is a mindset. This section 
breaks down both: the playbook that helped Glam Up scale to over a million users from May to 
November of 2024, and the mentality you actually need to survive building something from 
scratch. 
Playbook 
1. Pick an Industry with Platform-Native Virality 
 Before building anything, spend time researching what kinds of topics already 
perform well on the platform you plan to grow on. For example, beauty and dating 


--- PAGE 52 ---
51 
dominate TikTok because they’re visual, emotional, and easy to relate to. In contrast, 
crypto has huge traction on Twitter but barely makes a dent on TikTok (just search 
“crypto” in both platforms and you’ll see the difference). This means even the best crypto 
app might flop if you try to grow it on TikTok. 
Don’t just choose a “hot” industry—choose one that people are already creating 
content about on the platform you're targeting. TikTok has mass consumer reach, so if 
you want to go viral there, start with what people are already watching, posting, and 
sharing. 
2. Design for shareability, not just functionality (sometimes functionality doesn’t matter at 
all, RIP)
 
 Your app should look good in screenshots, videos, or someone’s story—not just 
function well behind the scenes. The most viral apps aren’t always the best 
products—they’re the ones people want to post about. That means designing features that 
are visual, satisfying, or surprising enough to make someone say, “Wait, I need to show 
this.” 
A perfectly functioning product that doesn’t trigger emotion or curiosity won’t go 
viral. A slightly janky app with a killer before/after moment or a trend-friendly result? 
That might. 
3. Don’t Marry a Marketing Strategy Until It Proves Itself 
 Different products go viral in different ways. Some blow up through faceless 
accounts. Others need a loud creator personality or a perfectly timed trend. The only way 
to know what works for your app is to try everything—UGC creators, influencers, meme 
pages, aesthetic montages, whatever.
 


--- PAGE 53 ---
52 
 The good news? If it works, you’ll know fast. TikTok/Instagram either picks it up 
or it doesn’t. So don’t sit around waiting to feel “ready.” Just try, fail fast, and when 
something hits, double down like crazy. 
4. Turn Marketing Into a System 
 Virality isn’t luck. Once you find a format that works, turn it into a playbook. 
Script the hooks. Standardize the visuals. Train your creators. Make content creation so 
repeatable that anyone on your team could do it. 
5. Scale 
Just focus on execution for this. 
Mindset 
1. Be Delusional 
 If you scroll through Twitter long enough, you’ll notice that every tech bro, 
founder, and early-stage builder swears by one thing — delusional faith. The idea that 
you have to believe irrationally hard in something before the world gives you a reason to. 
Accept the reality that at the beginning, nobody cares. You have no data, no traction, and 
probably no idea what you’re doing. You just have belief. And belief is what gets you to 
take the first 10 swings when logic says you shouldn’t. You don’t need validation to 
begin. You just need delusional faith. 
 
 Honestly, I think early-stage success is just a proxy for how delusional you’re 
willing to be, and whether you can back it up with grit, experimentation, and obsession. 
Your faith shows in your actions. If you really believe in what you’re building, it’ll show 
up in how many things you test, how hard you listen to users, and how many nights you 
spend trying to prove yourself right. Or wrong. 


--- PAGE 54 ---
53 
2. Growth Over Ego 
 Don’t worry about being right. Worry about learning fast. Treat every post, 
launch, and feature like an experiment. If it flops, learn. If it hits, scale. Growth happens 
when you stop making it about your ego. 
3. Question Everything (F*k What Everyone Else Says) 
 Just because something is “best practice” doesn’t mean it’s right for you. Most of 
the playbooks you see online are outdated, built for B2B SaaS or boomer consumer apps. 
The AI consumer space is new—and most of the people winning right now are Gen Z 
builders in their 20s, sometimes not even engineers. 
You’re not supposed to listen to VCs or the older generation when it comes to 
social media growth. It’s not that you know best — it’s that nobody knows. This space is 
too new for gatekeeping. If something doesn’t make sense, rip it apart. Build from first 
principles. That’s how you find your edge. 
4. 80-20 Rule 
 The 80/20 rule, also known as the Pareto Principle, states that roughly 80% of 
outcomes come from 20% of inputs. In startups, that means 80% of your growth, 
retention, or revenue usually stems from a small fraction of your inputs (app idea, product 
feature, paywall design, content formats, etc.) (Koch, 1999). The hard part is identifying 
that 20%—and having the discipline to ignore the rest. 
That 20% might be luck. But you can always try to maximize the surface area of 
your luck. Experiment fast, test wide, and stay close to your users. Luck favors builders 
who try more, ship more, and reflect more. In the early stages, it’s tempting to optimize 
everything: every screen, every flow, every feature. But virality often comes from one 


--- PAGE 55 ---
54 
hook that hits. Retention comes from one loop that sticks. Revenue comes from one flow 
that converts. 
5. Focus On Big Swings 
 Me and my co-founder always ask ourselves one question when we try something 
different: “Is this a big swing?” If it’s not, we don’t even bother trying. Big swings are 
bets that, if they work, change the game. A new content format. A viral growth mechanic. 
A paywall design that may convert way better. Not every swing will land. But when one 
does, it will do more for your app than 100 tiny tweaks ever could.
 
 It’s our way of checking whether we’re chasing the kind of idea that could 
actually change the trajectory of our app, or just over-optimizing a small feature that no 
one really cares about.
 
 Early on, it’s easy to get stuck tweaking button colors, rewriting tooltips, or 
reordering onboarding questions. But those things usually aren’t the reason your app 
grows. They’re not your 20%. They’re the noise. And when you’re building with limited 
time and energy, you need to swing big. 
6. Confidence Is Earned, Not Felt 
 You don’t need to feel confident to start. You just need to do the thing. Real 
confidence isn’t a mindset, it’s muscle memory. It comes from doing something enough 
times that it stops feeling scary. It’s built through small wins, daily reps, and putting 
yourself in motion even when you're unsure.
 
 Most people wait to feel ready. But the truth is, confidence shows up after you 
act—not before. If you already feel confident before doing the hard part, that’s not 


--- PAGE 56 ---
55 
confidence, it’s ego. And ego gets fragile fast when reality hits. Earned confidence, on the 
other hand, is resilient. It's built through reps, feedback, and showing up consistently. 
7. Confusion Is Normal. Keep Going Anyway 
 You’re going to feel lost half the time. That doesn’t mean you’re doing it wrong. 
It means you’re in the middle of figuring it out. Most people quit in confusion. Don’t be 
most people. 
8. Humility > Praise 
 Don’t try to make something impressive. Make something people actually want to 
talk about. It’s rarely the one that looks smartest, but the one that makes people feel 
something that blows up. Focus on that.
 
 On a personal note, I’ve never liked it when people say “I’m proud of you.” I 
know it’s meant with kindness, but it’s always felt off to me. Maybe it’s because I’ve 
wrestled with my own relationship to external validation. How can I feel validated by 
someone who doesn’t know wtf I went through to get here? How can I feel validated by 
someone else when I don’t even feel that way internally?
 
 That’s why I try to build from a place of humility, not performance. Not to 
impress anyone. Not to chase approval. But to make something out of nothing. 
Something that’s real. And in the process, maybe I’ll feel proud of it too.

11. Limitation 
This thesis is based on what we built, tested, and observed while scaling Glam Up, but 
like any case study grounded in a real-world startup, it comes with its own limitations. 
First, the data. When we first launched Glam Up, we didn’t have sophisticated analytics 
pipelines or detailed attribution tracking. We relied on tools like App Store Developer Console, 
PostHog and Superwall, but our setup wasn’t perfect — and many of the insights in this thesis 
are based on directional patterns, not airtight experiments. Some of the metrics, especially from 
the earliest growth periods, are rough estimates drawn from a combination of memory, 



--- PAGE 59 ---
58 
screenshots, and scattered event tracking. If we had set things up more rigorously from the 
beginning, the data would be cleaner — but that’s not how startups work.  
Second, this isn’t a universal playbook. What worked for Glam Up might not work for 
your app, especially if you’re in a different vertical, target a different demographic, or operate 
under different constraints. Beauty is an unusually visual, emotionally reactive category that 
thrives on platforms like TikTok. Our viral mechanics, pricing psychology, and content systems 
were deeply tied to the cultural context of Gen Z beauty trends in 2024. If you’re building a 
meditation app or a productivity tool, you probably shouldn’t copy this exactly. The principles 
might carry over, but the execution might differ. That said, part of why Glam Up’s strategy 
worked was that users ultimately felt it was worth paying for. Virality alone isn’t enough — if 
the product can’t convert attention into value, these approaches lose their edge. An app that can’t 
turn users into paying customers won’t benefit from the same playbook. 
Third, we weren’t trying to build a forever company. That shaped a lot of the decisions 
we made. We didn’t invest heavily in long-term retention. We didn’t spend months optimizing 
infrastructure or customer support. This was about speed, testing, and leverage. If you’re aiming 
for product-market fit in a mission-driven, long-horizon startup, our shortcuts might not apply. 
Some of what made Glam Up successful — like front-loaded monetization, minimal backend 
complexity, and emotionally reactive pricing — doesn’t necessarily translate to companies 
optimizing for LTV or user trust over years. 
Fourth, there are many things I didn’t cover. If I included every small test, weird insight, 
or backend decision, this paper would’ve been 1,000 pages long. So just know: there’s more. 
Things like App Store Optimization (ASO), strategies to “milk” content that already went viral, 
small UI tweaks that subtly boosted conversion. Those matter too. But I had to draw a line 


--- PAGE 60 ---
59 
somewhere. My goal here wasn’t to document everything, but to distill what actually moved the 
needle. If you’re building your own app, don’t stop at this thesis. Keep digging. 
Lastly, this paper is biased — by default. It’s written by someone who was too close to 
the product, too emotionally involved, and too busy building to track every experiment perfectly. 
I tried to be honest, but hindsight always adds clarity that wasn’t there in the moment. Some 
things worked because we planned them well. Others worked because we got lucky. I’m not 
always sure which was which. 
 
12. Conclusion 
Scaling a consumer app to 1 million users in six months wasn’t the result of a genius 
idea, a viral hack, or some magical timing. It was the outcome of relentless testing, fast feedback 
loops, emotional intuition, and building systems that could scale once something clicked. Glam 
Up didn’t start with a polished roadmap or product-market fit. It started with a phone call, a 
hunch, and a desperation to make something out of myself.  
Looking back, what made Glam Up work wasn’t any one feature or tactic—it was the 
combination of product decisions, marketing experiments, and viral design that all aligned 
around a single goal: grow fast by making something people want to share. Our referral system 
turned users into distribution channels. Our monetization play was emotional, not logical. Our 
content strategy wasn’t polished, it was persistent. And our mindset? Delusional, obsessive, and 
uncomfortably fast. 
But maybe the biggest lesson is this: building something that works doesn’t mean it’s 
something you want to keep building. Glam Up succeeded by all the external metrics: 
downloads, revenue, virality. But internally, I started craving chaos again. The mess of zero to 


--- PAGE 61 ---
60 
one. The curiosity of “will this even work?” So we pivoted. Not because Glam Up failed, but 
because it had already served its purpose. It was proof. And it became fuel. 
Now, I think differently about product, growth, and what it takes to go viral. I don’t 
believe in perfect playbooks or waiting for the right moment. I believe in motion, systems, and 
staying close to what users actually feel. If you’ve made it this far in this thesis, I hope you walk 
away not with a list of tactics to copy, but a deeper understanding of how viral apps are built in 
real life—not in theory. And more than that, I hope it gives you the confidence to just start. 
Because the truth is: nobody really knows what they’re doing. You just have to build 
anyway.
